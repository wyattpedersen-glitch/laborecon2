1. Wage posting: the one new move

Steps

  1. Write profit as a function of the wage: \(\pi(w)=p\,y\big(L^s(w)\big)-w\,L^s(w)\).
  2. Differentiate with respect to \(w\): \(p\,y'\,L^{s\prime}-L-w\,L^{s\prime}=0\).
  3. Divide by \(L^{s\prime}\) and use \(\eta=\frac{w}{L}L^{s\prime}\): \(p\,y'=w+\frac{w}{\eta}=w\big(1+\frac1\eta\big)\).
  4. So \(w=p\,y'\cdot\dfrac{1}{1+1/\eta}=\text{MRPL}\cdot\dfrac{\eta}{1+\eta}\).

Why it works. A competitive firm thinks it can hire as many workers as it wants at the going wage, so its cost of one more worker is just \(w\). A wage-posting firm knows that to attract one more worker it has to raise the wage for everyone it already employs. So its marginal cost of labor is \(w\) plus that raise times all its workers: \(w+L\,w'(L)=w(1+1/\eta)\). It sets that marginal cost equal to MRPL, and pays only what the supply curve requires: less than MRPL.

How to read η. \(\eta\) is how many percent of workers leave when the firm cuts pay by 1%. Big \(\eta\) means workers leave easily, so the firm can't push the wage down much. Small \(\eta\) means they stay, so the firm marks the wage down a lot.

Mnemonic

"Eta over eta-plus-one is the slice you keep." \(\eta=1\): you keep half. \(\eta=4\): 80%. \(\eta=9\): 90%. \(\eta\to\infty\): all of it (competition). Datta's \(\eta\approx4.6\) gives 82%; Staiger's \(\eta\approx0.1\) gives 9%.

2. Elasticities: where η comes from

From a supply curve

Take logs, read the coefficient on \(\log w\).

  • \(L^s=Nw^\alpha\): \(\log L=\log N+\alpha\log w\), so \(\eta=\alpha\), constant.
  • \(L^s=N(w-w_H)\) (Midterm 1 Q1): \(\eta=\frac{dL}{dw}\frac{w}{L}=\frac{w}{w-w_H}\). Not constant.
  • General: \(\eta=\frac{\partial\log L}{\partial\log w}=L^{s\prime}(w)\cdot\frac{w}{L}\).

From data

  • Basic: \(\eta=\%\Delta L/\%\Delta w\).
  • Long run: \(L=R/S\Rightarrow\eta=\varepsilon_R-\varepsilon_S\). "Hires minus quits." Separation elasticity is negative, so subtracting it adds. Datta: \(3-(-1.6)=4.6\).
  • Survey (SHED): quit share 47% → 51% after a 1% cut: \(\frac{0.51-0.47}{0.47}=8.5\%\), so the quit elasticity is −8.5.
  • IV: \[\eta=\frac{\text{effect of instrument on }\log L}{\text{effect of instrument on }\log w}\].

Why OLS of \(\log L\) on \(\log w\) fails. Firms move along their supply curve only when demand shifts. If the wage variation comes from supply shocks, you trace out demand instead. You need an instrument that moves the firm's wage without moving labor supply directly: a demand shock or an institutional wage change (Staiger, Datta).

3. Posting vs competition, full model (the practice question)

Steps

  1. Supply. Work iff utility from working ≥ not. Get the reservation wage, then \(L^s(w)=N\,P[w^r_i\le w]\). With \(P[a\le x]=x^\eta\): \(L^s=Nw^\eta\).
  2. MRPL. Differentiate production: \(y=AL^\rho\Rightarrow\text{MRPL}=\rho AL^{\rho-1}\).
  3. Competitive. \(w=\text{MRPL}(L^s(w))\): \(w=\rho A(Nw^\eta)^{\rho-1}\). Collect powers of \(w\): \(w^{1+\eta-\eta\rho}=\rho AN^{\rho-1}\).
  4. Posting. Same equation with MRPL shrunk by \(\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}\): \(w^{1+\eta-\eta\rho}=\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}\rho AN^{\rho-1}\). Or maximize \(A(Nw^\eta)^\rho-wNw^\eta\) over \(w\) directly, as the answer key does; you get the same thing.
  5. Employment in either case: plug the wage into \(L^s=Nw^\eta\).

Why step 4 works. With constant \(\eta\), the posting condition \(w=\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}\text{MRPL}\) is the competitive condition with \(A\) multiplied by \(\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}\). So \[w_{post}=\big(\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}\big)^{\frac{1}{1+\eta-\eta\rho}}w_{comp}\].

Mnemonic

"Posting is competition with a shrunk A." Solve the competitive case once, then redo it with \(A\to A\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}\).

Traps
  • Posting chooses the wage, so you differentiate with respect to \(w\), not \(L\). If you choose \(L\) instead, use marginal cost \(w(L)+Lw'(L)\); both routes agree.
  • With constant returns (\(y=AL\)), MRPL \(=A\) is constant, so \(w_{post}=A\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}\) immediately.
  • Exponents: \(1+\eta-\eta\rho=1-\eta(\rho-1)\). The answer key writes it the second way. Same thing.

4. Bargaining, and posting vs bargaining

Steps

  1. Who is hired? Everyone whose reservation wage is below MRPL. With MRPL \(=1\) and \(v_i\in[0,1]\): everyone. This is the efficient employment level.
  2. What do they earn? L7 protocol: their reservation wage, \(w_i=w^r_i\). L8 Nash protocol: \(w_i=\beta\,\text{MRPL}+(1-\beta)w^r_i\).
  3. Average wage \(=\int w_i\,f(v)\,dv\). With \(F(x)=x^\alpha\): \(f(v)=\alpha v^{\alpha-1}\) and \(E[v]=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\).
  4. Compare to posting: posting pays everyone \(\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\) and hires \(N(\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha})^\alpha\).

Why bargaining is efficient. The firm pays each worker separately, so hiring one more worker doesn't raise anyone else's pay. Its marginal cost is just that worker's reservation wage, so it hires everyone worth hiring. The firm still takes most of the surplus; it just doesn't need to restrict employment to do so.

The spillovers row is the empirical test. Give one worker a better outside option: under bargaining their wage rises, under posting nothing happens. Give everyone better options: under posting the wage rises. Townsend & Allan found exactly the posting pattern.

5. Minimum wages

Steps

  1. Find \(w_{comp}\) (supply = MRPL) and \(w_{post}\) (markdown).
  2. Place the minimum wage \(\underline w\) in one of three regions:
    • \(\underline w\le w_{post}\): doesn't bind anywhere. Nothing changes.
    • \(w_{post}<\underline w\le w_{comp}\): competitive model unaffected (the wage was already higher). Posting: employment rises to \(L^s(\underline w)\).
    • \(\underline w>w_{comp}\): both models land on the demand curve: \(\text{MRPL}(L)=\underline w\). Employment falls as \(\underline w\) rises.
  3. Employment-maximizing minimum wage under posting: exactly \(w_{comp}\).

Why a floor can raise employment. The posting firm held back hiring because each extra worker meant raising everyone's wage. With a floor, it must pay \(\underline w\) to everyone anyway, so up to the point where supply runs out, an extra worker costs just \(\underline w\). Marginal cost becomes flat, and the firm hires everyone willing to work at \(\underline w\) as long as MRPL \(\ge\underline w\).

Mnemonic

"Between the wages, jobs go up." Below the posted wage: nothing. Between posted and competitive: jobs up. Above competitive: jobs down. The peak is at the competitive wage.

6. Pay transparency

Steps

  1. Before (bargaining): \(w_i=\beta+(1-\beta)v_i\), everyone with \(v_i\le1\) works, average \(=\beta+(1-\beta)\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\).
  2. After (posting): \(w=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\) for everyone (\(\eta=\alpha\)), employment \(N\big(\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\big)^\alpha\).
  3. Compare. Inequality falls to zero; the average wage falls by \(\beta\big(1-\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\big)=\frac{\beta}{1+\alpha}\); employment falls.

Why transparency means posting. If everyone can see what everyone else earns, a worker paid less will demand the higher wage, or unequal pay hurts morale and productivity (Breza, Kaur & Shamdasani). Either way the firm ends up paying one wage.

Mnemonic

"Transparency turns bargaining into posting: equal, lower, fewer." Data: wages fell (Cullen & Pakzad-Hurson: −2%), the gender gap shrank (Baker et al.), but employment didn't fall.

7. Two groups (HW3 Q2)

  • Separate wages: each group gets its own markdown: \(w_g=\frac{\eta_g}{1+\eta_g}\text{MRPL}\). Less elastic group → lower wage.
  • Common wage: \(L=L_1+L_2\) so \[\eta=\frac{w}{L}(L_1'+L_2')=\frac{L_1}{L}\eta_1+\frac{L_2}{L}\eta_2\]. "Weighted elasticities, one wage."
  • Why women might be less elastic, through "Picky, Blind, Few": stronger preferences over commute or flexibility (picky), fewer suitable employers nearby (few), job info through narrower networks (blind).

8. HHI

\(\text{HHI}=\sum_f s_f^2\). Equal-sized firms: \(\text{HHI}=1/N\), so \(1/\text{HHI}\) is the "equivalent number of firms." Multiply by 10,000 when the paper does. A merger of firms with shares \(s_a,s_b\) raises HHI by \(2s_as_b\).

Lecture 6 · Do employers have market power?

So far we assumed firms take the wage as given, so the wage equals MRPL. This lecture asks why a firm would ignore that hiring more requires paying more, shows that firms face surprisingly inelastic labor supply, and gives three reasons why.

1. The markdown

A firm that knows it faces an upward-sloping supply curve \(w(L)\) chooses \(L\) to maximize \(p\,y(L)-w(L)L\):

\[ p\,y'(L)-Lw'(L)-w=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad w=p\,y'(L)-Lw'(L). \]

Since \(Lw'(L)=w/\eta\) where \(\eta\equiv\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}\frac{w}{L}\):

\[ w=\underbrace{p\,y'(L)}_{\text{MRPL}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{1}{1+\frac1\eta}}_{\text{markdown}}. \]
  • \(\eta\approx\infty\): \(w\approx\) MRPL. Pretending firms take wages as given is fine.
  • \(\eta\approx0\): \(w\) far below MRPL.
  • Traditional view: many firms, so a firm paying slightly less would lose all its workers, so \(\eta\) must be huge. This lecture's evidence says no.
  • In labor, "monopsony" doesn't mean one employer; it means each employer has some wage-setting power.

2. Estimating the elasticity facing a firm

Why not OLS of \(\log L_f\) on \(\log w_f\)? If wage variation reflects supply shocks rather than demand shocks, OLS traces out the demand curve, not supply. You need an instrument that is independent of supply shocks, doesn't itself affect supply, but does move the wage. Two candidates: labor demand shocks and institutional wage changes. Both L6 papers use the second.

Staiger, Spetz & Phibbs (2010): VA nurses

Before 1991 the VA paid nurses on a national scale, so VA hospitals in high-wage markets paid too little (SF: $14.00 vs $16.30). The Nurse Pay Act (1990) tied VA wages to local market wages from April 1991. The 1990 wage gap predicts the VA raise (slope ≈ 0.83). Using that as an instrument, the elasticity of RN supply to a hospital is roughly 0.1: nurses paid about 9% of MRPL.

Problems: (1) exclusion: the mandated raise also pushed up competitors' wages (non-VA coefficient ≈ 0.09–0.34), so you have to model how supply depends on own and rivals' wages; (2) very short run (1990 vs 1992).

Datta (2024): UK service firm

Large UK firm, ≈18,800 workers, 362 establishments. Two instruments: (1) living-wage accreditation of local councils whose contracts require the firm to pay the living wage (raises entry-level wages ≈ 6%); (2) whether the posted wage happened to include the mandatory 12.07% holiday top-up, depending on which HR staffer wrote the posting (recruitment only).

Recruitment elasticity ≈ 3, separation elasticity ≈ −0.08/0.05 = −1.6, so total ≈ 4.6 and workers get about 80% of MRPL. Separation elasticity grows with tenure.

Long-run elasticity: "hires minus quits." \(L_t=L_{t-1}+R_t-S_{t-1}L_{t-1}\). In steady state \(S_tL_t=R_t\), so \(L=R/S\), \(\log L=\log R-\log S\), and \[ \frac{\partial\log L}{\partial\log w}=\underbrace{\frac{\partial\log R}{\partial\log w}}_{\text{recruitment}}-\underbrace{\frac{\partial\log S}{\partial\log w}}_{\text{separation}}. \] Many papers estimate one half and double it, assuming \(\varepsilon_R=-\varepsilon_S\) (ignoring unemployment, a recruit from one firm is a separation from another). Datta estimates both.

Survey route (SHED)

"How likely would you be to look for another job or leave if your employer cut your pay by 0 / 1 / 5 / 10%?" Somewhat or very likely: 47% at 0%, 51% at 1%, ≈67% at 5%, ≈76% at 10%. Quit elasticity at 1%: \(\frac{0.51-0.47}{0.47}=8.5\%\) for a −1% wage, so −8.5. Caveat: pay cuts are rare, so respondents may read one as a signal of layoffs.

4. Concentration

\(\text{HHI}=\sum_f s_f^2\): a share-weighted average of shares. Equal shares: \(\text{HHI}=1/N\), the "equivalent number of employers." Often reported ×10,000.

Prager & Schmitt (2021): hospital mergers

Hospital mergers 2000–2010. Treated: commuting zones with exactly one merger. Control: zones with none. DiD with two-way fixed effects: \[\ln w_{imt}=\delta_i+\tau_t+\alpha\,\text{post}_{mt}+\beta X_{imt}+\varepsilon_{imt}\].

  • Average merger: no wage effect. Top quartile of ΔHHI (HHI 4,580 → 7,344): skilled wages −4.1%, nursing/pharmacy −7.0%, unskilled ≈ 0.
  • No effect for out-of-market mergers (placebo) and no change in local unemployment, population or income.
  • Smaller effects where nurse unionization is high.
  • Employment didn't fall. Under posting, a firm with more power cuts wages by moving down its supply curve, so employment should fall. Wages falling without employment falling fits bargaining better. Unskilled workers unaffected: they have outside options beyond hospitals.

Parallel trends worry: merging hospitals might be in markets already in decline. Pre-trends are mostly flat (unskilled wages wobble pre-merger) and treated hospitals are bigger, in bigger and richer zones.

Azar, Marinescu, Steinbaum & Taska (2020)

HHI from online job postings at 6-digit occupation × commuting zone × quarter: mean unweighted HHI 4,378, but employment-weighted only 1,361. Questions to raise: is that the right market definition, and should concentration be measured in jobs or postings?

Interpretation questions to be ready for
  • Is Staiger's 0.1 believable? It implies wages at 9% of MRPL. Likely biased down: competitors' wages rose too (exclusion), and two years is short.
  • Why use an institutional wage change as an instrument? It moves the firm's wage without being a supply shock.
  • Why did mergers lower nurse wages but not employment? Bargaining, not posting.
  • Does monopsony require one employer? No: picky preferences and search frictions give market power even with many firms.

Lecture 7 · Wage-setting

Firms have power to set wages. But how? Either they post one take-it-or-leave-it wage, or they bargain with each worker. The answer changes predictions for efficiency, inequality and policy, and the evidence mostly favors posting.

1. Common setup

  • One firm \(F\), workers \(i\in[0,N]\), decreasing-returns production \(y_F(L)\), output price \(p\).
  • Each worker works at \(F\) or at an outside option paying \(w_O\). Utility \(\log C\) at the outside option and \(\log C-v_i\) at \(F\), with \(v_i\) the relative distaste for \(F\).
  • Works at \(F\) iff \[\log w_{i,F}-v_i\ge\log w_O\iff v_i\le\log w_{i,F}-\log w_O\].

HW3 and the L8 transparency model use a simpler version: utility \(C-ev_i\), no outside income (\(w_O=0\)), so work iff \(w\ge v_i\) and the reservation wage is just \(v_i\).

2. Model 1: wage posting

The firm picks one wage; workers accept or reject. Supply is \(L^s(w)=N\,P[v_i\le\log w-\log w_O]\).

\[ \max_w\ p\,y_F(L^s(w))-wL^s(w)\ \Rightarrow\ p\,y_F'L^{s\prime}-wL^{s\prime}-L=0. \]

Substitute \(L^{s\prime}=\eta L/w\), divide by \(\eta L\), multiply by \(w\): \(p\,y_F'-w-\frac{w}{\eta}=0\), so

\[ w=p\,y_F'(L)\cdot\frac{1}{1+\frac1\eta}. \]

Same markdown as L6. Graph: hire where marginal cost = MRPL, pay what the supply curve requires (see Attack plans §1).

3. Model 2: bargaining

  • The firm's willingness to pay for each worker is MRPL \(p\,y_F'\). Worker \(i\) is willing at their reservation wage \(w^r_i\).
  • Workers with \(w^r_i>p\,y_F'\) are never employed. For the rest, a bargaining protocol splits the surplus \(p\,y_F'-w^r_i\).
  • L7 protocol: the firm keeps all the surplus; each worker gets exactly \(w^r_i\). Think of a firm that knows each worker's reservation wage and makes a targeted offer just big enough.
  • Graph: bargained wages trace the distribution of reservation wages up to MRPL; employment is where supply hits MRPL.

Both pay less than MRPL. Seeing different wages for workers at one firm doesn't prove bargaining: are they really doing the same job?

5. Evidence: how are wages set?

Surveys

Hall & Krueger (2008): 36% bargained over pay, 32% knew pay exactly at interview, 47% said the employer learned their previous pay. Caldwell & Harmon: bargaining ≈ 20% in manual, assembly, service and office jobs; ≈ 40–60% for craftsmen, technicians, professionals and managers.

Limits: is the bargaining quantitatively important? And many firms differentiate pay without explicit bargaining: 78% of German firms pay equally productive workers in the same position differently (Caldwell, Haegele & Heining).

Cullen, Pakzad-Hurson & Perez-Truglia (2025)

RCT on levels.fyi users looking for jobs (tech). Treatment: a message that 42% negotiate and 85% of those succeed. Many had low priors about both.

Intent to negotiate +3.6pp; actually negotiated +7.3pp (53.7% → 61.0%); improved offer +5.7pp. Larger for low-prior and for women (negotiated +16.8pp). About half who negotiated got more; average raise among them 13.9%; average wage effect of the treatment ≈ +1%. Negotiation coaching had no effect. So bargaining exists and matters a bit, especially in tech.

Townsend & Allan (2025): migrants in New Zealand

Essential Skills visas tie a migrant to one job, so a shock to restrictions is a shock to outside options. Two tests:

  1. Individual shock. Migrants who move from a restricted visa to residence switch employers much more (monthly new-employer rate ≈ 1% → 3%), but their earnings track same-firm, same-occupation controls exactly. Hours unchanged (rules out better conditions instead of pay). To rule out applying for residence in anticipation of individual shocks: visa lotteries (Pacific Access, Samoan Quota): winning raises residence by ≈ 70pp and employer switching by ≈ 10–14pp, with no earnings gain.
  2. Market shock. When an occupation is put on the Essential Skills in Demand list, all migrants in it become unrestricted. DiD: earnings +2.8% (±2.0), for migrants and residents; more workers at new firms; no change in matched Australian occupations. Assumption: list changes are independent of labor demand/supply changes (policymakers do respond to shocks, but some changes were for other reasons, e.g. occupation size).

Conclusion: one worker's outside option doesn't move their wage; everyone's does. That's posting. Mnemonic: "Me alone: no raise. All of us: raise."

Caldwell & Oehlsen (2026): Uber and Lyft (HW3 Q3, not in slides)

Uber randomly offered subsets of drivers higher pay rates; some drivers could also drive for Lyft. Randomized offers instrument realized hourly earnings (realized pay depends on when and where drivers choose to work). Access to one competing platform nearly doubles the elasticity of supply to Uber, cutting the implied markdown from 68% to about 50%. Women are about twice as elastic as men to the market, but no less elastic to their employer.

Source: the paper's NBER abstract, as these details weren't in your slides. Cross-check against your HW3 answers.

6. Which model is right?

  • Probably varies by country, occupation and firm, but for most workers posting fits better (Townsend & Allan; low bargaining shares in surveys).
  • Hospital nurses (Prager & Schmitt) and tech workers (Cullen et al.) show bargaining-like features.
  • Why don't firms bargain more? Our protocol (worker gets exactly their reservation wage) is the most favorable to firms; other protocols make bargaining less attractive. Firms probably don't know reservation wages. Pay inequality lowers productivity (Breza, Kaur & Shamdasani).
Interpretation questions to be ready for
  • Which model does Townsend & Allan support? Posting: individual outside options don't matter, market-wide ones do.
  • Which does Prager & Schmitt support? Bargaining: wages fell without employment falling.
  • Does wage dispersion within a firm reject posting? Not necessarily: different jobs, different productivity.
  • Is bargaining efficient? Yes, under our protocol: all workers with \(w^r<\) MRPL are hired. Posting isn't.
  • HW3 Q3c: is the gender pay gap due to different markdowns? Caldwell & Oehlsen find women no less elastic to their employer, so different markdowns don't explain it there.

Lecture 8 · Labor market regulation

The wage-setting models applied to three regulations: minimum wages, noncompete bans and pay transparency. For each, theory first, then evidence.

1. Minimum wages

The real federal minimum wage fell from ≈$15 (1968, in 2026 $) to ≈$7.25 today; the population-weighted average across states is ≈$12, because states raised their own. The main argument against minimum wages is that they cut employment. True in the competitive model; not necessarily with market power.

Competitive

\(w^{comp}\ge\underline w\) and \(L^{comp}=\arg\max_L\{y_f(L)-w^{comp}L\}\); markets clear unless the minimum binds. A binding minimum puts you on the MRPL curve, so raising it lowers employment.

Wage posting

\(\max_{w,L}\{y_f(L)-wL\}\) s.t. \(L\le L^s(w)\) and \(w\ge\underline w\). A minimum between the posted and competitive wage flattens marginal cost, so the firm hires everyone willing at \(\underline w\). A higher minimum can raise employment.

Takeaway: the employment effect is theoretically ambiguous. It depends on whether employers have market power and how high the minimum is.

Horton (2025 AER): online platform RCT

Gig platform where workers bid and firms pick. Minimum bids of $2, $3 or $4/hour, randomized across firms. Hiring fell (≈ −6% at $4), only about 10% of what you'd expect if every job below the minimum vanished. Hours fell up to ≈25%. Wages among hires +18% to +44%. Firms substituted toward more productive workers: hires had higher past wages and earnings, more US hires, fewer from Bangladesh.

Cengiz, Dube, Lindner & Zipperer (2019 QJE)

DiD across 138 state minimum-wage increases, 1979–2016. Compare employment counts by wage bin just below and just above the new minimum, against counterfactual states. Missing jobs below \(\Delta b=-1.8\%\) of employment, excess jobs just above \(\Delta a=+2.1\%\): no net job loss (affected employment +2.8%, n.s.; affected wages +6.8%). Flat pre-trends; ≈40% of the wage gain is spillovers above the minimum. Same for teens, less-educated workers and tradeable sectors.

Which model? No job loss fits posting. But posting says output rises, so prices should fall; Harasztosi & Lindner find firms pass much of the cost into higher prices. So maybe the competitive model with very inelastic labor demand. Dustmann et al. find minimum wages shift workers from small to large firms (perhaps large firms have more power). US minimums probably have small or zero employment effects; city minimums may bite more (firms can cross city lines); a high enough minimum must cut jobs; the ideal level varies by sector (other countries use sectoral bargaining).

Parallel trends here means: employment in each wage bin in states raising their minimum would have evolved like employment in that bin in other states.

2. Noncompete agreements

Contracts that bar working for competitors after leaving. Justified as protecting customers and encouraging firm-sponsored training. Unenforceable in many states; California now bans even including them. About 18% of US workers have one (Starr, Prescott & Bishara). Even unpaid interns get asked to sign them.

"Cage versus comp." Cage: a noncompete shrinks outside options and so reservation wages; under posting or bargaining, wages fall. Comp: if workers foresee lower future earnings, they demand more to sign (a compensating differential); wages rise. Evidence says the cage wins.

Johnson, Lavetti & Lipsitz (2025 JPE)

State-by-year 0–1 enforceability score built from court rulings and laws (e.g. Pennsylvania 1995: enforceable even if fired, +0.1). Many changes in both directions. DiD. Changes are uncorrelated with state politics and economic conditions. Moving from 0 to 1 cuts earnings ≈ 11% (event study: growing over 5 years, flat pre-trends); bigger for more-educated and high-use occupations and industries; job-to-job mobility falls in high-use industries; spillovers across state borders. Also: less innovation (fewer knowledge flows), weak evidence of more training.

Cowgill, Freiberg & Starr (2026 QJE)

Field experiment with ≈14,000 freelance recruiters offered short-term resume-review work by Firm A. Randomized the wage ($25 vs $65) and whether the contract had a salient, hidden or no noncompete (15,000 got no offer). Salient: fewer opened (−7.9pp) and signed (−2.3pp), worth ≈$15–44 in wage terms; hidden: little effect (most spent seconds on that page). Later, Firm B tried to hire the same workers: noncompetes cut moving to B only after Firm A's reminder letter. Secret-sharing was rare and unaffected.

Why don't noncompetes come with higher pay? Many workers don't notice them (hidden ≈ no effect), and they bite only when enforced or salient.

3. Pay transparency

Horizontal transparency laws let workers discuss pay or require firms to publish it. Motivation: shrink the gender gap by letting women see when they're underpaid. Model: transparency shifts the firm from bargaining to posting.

Model (L8 version)

One firm, \(y=AL\), \(p=A=1\). Worker utility \(C-ev_i\), no other income, \(F(x)=P[v_i<x]=x^\alpha\). Work iff \(w_{i,F}\ge v_i\).

  • Before (Nash bargaining): \(w_i=\beta\,pA+(1-\beta)v_i\) with bargaining strength \(\beta\in(0,1)\). \(\beta=0\): reservation wage (L7). \(\beta=1\): MRPL.
  • Average: \[\int_0^1(\beta+(1-\beta)v)\,\alpha v^{\alpha-1}dv=\beta+(1-\beta)\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\].
  • After (posting): \(\eta=\frac{\partial}{\partial\log w}\log(Nw^\alpha)=\alpha\), so \(w^{post}=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\).
  • If \(\beta>0\): \(\beta+(1-\beta)\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}>\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\). Transparency reduces inequality but also reduces average wages (and, in the model, employment).

Baker et al. (2023 AEJ: Applied)

Canadian provinces required universities to publish names and salaries above a threshold (Ontario and Manitoba 1996, BC 1996/2002, Nova Scotia 2012, Alberta 2015, Newfoundland 2016). DiD: treated = faculty with at least one coworker above the threshold at reform; controls = same province, lower-paid departments (province × year × gender fixed effects). Gender gap shrinks ≈ 1.2–2pp, mostly because men's salaries fell; largest at the 25th–50th percentiles.

Cullen & Pakzad-Hurson (2023 ECMA)

13 US "Right of Workers to Talk" laws, 2000–2016, protecting the right to discuss and ask about pay, with penalties. Share prohibited from discussing pay fell faster in adopting states (33% → 10%). Event-study DiD: wages fell ≈ 2% for men and women alike, more in low-union occupations (more individual bargaining). Employment didn't change.

Fits the model on wages. Misses on employment; but our model has one firm. If workers move easily between firms but rarely between work and non-work, employment needn't fall.

Interpretation questions to be ready for
  • Is Cengiz et al. consistent with competition or posting? Posting (no job loss), with the price pass-through caveat.
  • Horton found employment fell. Does that reject posting? Not necessarily: a $4 floor may be above the competitive wage for some jobs. And firms substituted to better workers, a margin our model ignores.
  • Why don't noncompetes raise wages as compensating differentials? Workers don't notice them (Cowgill et al.); the "cage" effect dominates (Johnson et al.).
  • Why did transparency cut men's pay rather than raise women's? In the model, moving to posting lowers the average; firms equalize by cutting the top.

Lecture 6 · Market power

(a) Is there monopsony? VA nurses (Staiger, Spetz & Phibbs 2010)

IV
Setting
Registered nurses at Veterans Affairs hospitals. Before 1991 VA pay followed a national scale, so VA hospitals in high-wage cities underpaid (SF: $14.00 vs $16.30). The Nurse Pay Act of 1990 tied VA wages to local market wages from April 1991, with funds for the higher wage bills.
Design
IV: the 1990 gap between local market and VA wages predicts the size of the mandated raise (slope ≈ 0.83), and that predicted raise instruments the hospital's wage change. Assumptions: the gap is unrelated to supply shocks, and affects employment only through the hospital's own wage. Exclusion is doubtful: the raise also pushed up competitors' wages (non-VA hospitals' wages rose too), so you need to model supply as a function of own and rivals' wages. Also very short run (1990 vs 1992).
Findings
Very inelastic supply to the firm: \(\eta\approx0.1\) (2SLS estimates ≈ 0–0.2). Implied markdown \(\frac{1}{1+1/0.1}\approx0.09\): nurses paid ≈ 9% of MRPL. Implausibly low, likely biased down.
Model
Strong monopsony: the markdown model with small \(\eta\).

(b) The measure of monopsony (Datta 2024)

IV
Setting
A large UK service-sector firm: ≈18,800 workers, 362 establishments, median £10.20/hr, many on contracts with local councils.
Design
Estimates recruitment and separation elasticities separately, with two instruments: (1) living-wage accreditation: when a council becomes accredited, new or renewed contracts require the living wage (entry-level wages +6%); (2) whether the posted wage happened to include the mandatory 12.07% holiday top-up, which depended on which HR staffer wrote the posting (recruitment only). Assumptions: accreditation timing and the HR quirk are unrelated to local supply shocks and affect hiring and quits only via wages. Plausible: the top-up was always paid, only its display varied; accreditation is a council decision. Pre-trends flat.
Findings
Recruitment elasticity ≈ 3; separation ≈ −0.08/0.05 ≈ −1.6; total \(\eta\approx3+1.6=4.6\) ("hires minus quits"). Markdown \(\frac{1}{1+1/4.6}\approx0.8\): workers get ≈ 80% of MRPL. Separation elasticity grows in magnitude with tenure.
Model
Wage posting with moderate market power: meaningful, not extreme.

(d) Employer consolidation and wages: hospital mergers (Prager & Schmitt 2021)

DiD
Setting
US hospital mergers 2000–2010. Hospitals are big employers of nurses and pharmacists with few local alternatives.
Design
DiD with two-way fixed effects: treated = commuting zones with exactly one hospital merger; control = zones with none. \[\ln w_{imt}=\delta_i+\tau_t+\alpha\,\text{post}_{mt}+\beta X+\varepsilon\]. Assumption: parallel trends; merging markets would have followed the same wage path. Checks: event studies (mostly flat pre-trends), placebo out-of-market mergers (no effect), no change in local unemployment, population or income. Worry: treated hospitals are bigger and in bigger, richer zones; mergers might target declining markets.
Findings
Average merger: no effect. Top quartile of ΔHHI (HHI 4,580 → 7,344): skilled wages −4.1%, nursing and pharmacy −7.0%, unskilled ≈ 0. Smaller effects with more unionization. No change in employment.
Model
Concentration ("Few") gives market power. Unskilled unaffected because they have options outside hospitals. Wages falling without employment falling fits bargaining, not posting.

Lecture 7 · Wage-setting

(c) Outside options and labor supply: gig economy (Caldwell & Oehlsen 2026)

RCT used as IV
Setting
Uber drivers, who choose their own hours. Uber ran experiments offering random subsets of male and female drivers higher pay. Some drivers could also drive for Lyft, others couldn't.
Design
IV: randomized pay offers instrument realized hourly earnings. OLS fails because realized earnings depend on drivers' choices (when and where to drive) and demand shocks. Assumptions: relevance (offers raise pay); as good as random (randomized ✓); exclusion (the offer affects hours only through pay, not e.g. as a signal or through attention); monotonicity. Plausible. Lyft comparison needs more: access to Lyft must be unrelated to other drivers' traits that affect their elasticity.
Findings
Access to one competing platform nearly doubles the elasticity of supply to Uber; the implied markdown falls from 68% to about 50%. Women are about twice as elastic as men to the market, but no less elastic to their employer.
Model
Markdown model: better outside options mean higher \(\eta\) and a smaller markdown. On gender: no evidence of different markdowns by sex, so it doesn't explain the gender gap here (HW3 Q3c).

Details from the paper's NBER abstract; this paper wasn't in the lecture slides. Cross-check with your HW3 answers.

(e) Pushing the envelope: salary negotiations (Cullen, Pakzad-Hurson & Perez-Truglia 2025)

RCT
Setting
Tech job seekers recruited through levels.fyi (a pay-comparison site). Many underestimated how common and how successful negotiation is.
Design
RCT: treated participants get a message that 42% of Americans negotiate and 85% of those succeed (plus negotiation lessons). Assumption: random assignment, so groups are comparable; outcomes observed only for those who report offers (Lee bounds handle selective reporting). Plausible.
Findings
Intent to negotiate +3.6pp; actually negotiated +7.3pp (53.7% → 61.0%); improved offer +5.7pp. Bigger for low priors and for women (negotiated +16.8pp). About half who negotiated got more, averaging +13.9%. Average wage effect of the treatment ≈ +1%. Coaching had no effect.
Model
Some bargaining exists and moves wages, at least for tech workers. The modest average effect fits a world that is mostly posting.

(f) Restricting migrants' job options (Townsend & Allan 2025)

Event study · lottery IV · DiD
Setting
New Zealand's Essential Skills visa ties a migrant to one job, only where no New Zealanders are available, unless the occupation is on the Essential Skills in Demand list.
Design
(1) Individual shock: migrants moving from the tied visa to residence vs controls in the same firm and occupation. Concern that they get better conditions instead of pay: hours unchanged. Concern that they apply for residence anticipating personal shocks: use random visa lotteries (Pacific Access, Samoan Quota; 6,501 entries from 3,891 people). (2) Market shock: DiD on occupations added to the shortage list. Assumption: list changes independent of changes in labor demand and supply. Policymakers do respond to shocks, but some changes were for other reasons (e.g. occupation size). Matched Australian occupations show no change, supporting it.
Findings
Individual: residence makes migrants switch employers much more (≈ 1% → 3% a month; lottery win: +70pp residence, +10–14pp switching) but no earnings gain. Market: listing raises earnings ≈ 2.8% for migrants and residents, with more workers at new firms.
Model
Wage posting. Under posting the wage depends on everyone's outside options (through \(\eta\)), not one worker's. "Me alone: no raise. All of us: raise."

(g) Price floors and employer preferences: minimum wage experiment (Horton 2025)

RCT
Setting
An online gig platform where workers bid on jobs and firms choose.
Design
RCT: minimum bids of $2, $3 or $4/hour (or none), randomized across firms. Assumption: random assignment; no spillovers between treated and control firms on the same platform. Plausible, but it's a partial-equilibrium, small-firm setting.
Findings

Model
Mostly competitive: a binding floor reduces labor bought. The substitution to better workers is a margin our one-type models leave out.

(h) Minimum wages and low-wage jobs (Cengiz, Dube, Lindner & Zipperer 2019)

DiD (bunching)
Setting
138 US state minimum-wage increases, 1979–2016.
Design
DiD by wage bin: count jobs just below and just above the new minimum in treated states, against counterfactual states. Assumption: parallel trends bin by bin: employment in each wage bin in raising states would have evolved like in other states. Flat pre-trends support it.

Model
Consistent with wage posting. Caveat: posting predicts output up and prices down, but firms pass costs into higher prices (Harasztosi & Lindner), so maybe competitive with very inelastic demand.

(i) Legal restrictions on worker mobility (Johnson, Lavetti & Lipsitz 2025)

DiD
Setting
US states 1991–2014. Noncompete enforceability changes often, mostly through court decisions, in both directions.
Design
A 0–1 state-year enforceability score (e.g. Pennsylvania 1995: enforceable even if fired, +0.1). DiD/event study using changes in the score. Assumption: parallel trends, i.e. law changes aren't driven by state economic trends. Changes are uncorrelated with politics, unemployment and other state characteristics; pre-trends flat.
Findings
Moving from unenforceable to fully enforceable cuts earnings ≈ 11%, more for educated workers and high-use occupations and industries; job-to-job mobility falls in high-use industries; effects spill across state borders. Also: less innovation; weak evidence of more training.

"Cage beats comp": noncompetes shrink outside options, lowering wages under posting or bargaining.

(j) Clause and effect: noncompete field experiment (Cowgill, Freiberg & Starr 2026)

RCT
Setting
Freelance HR recruiters; two finance firms. Phase 1: Firm A offers ≈14,000 recruiters resume-review work. Phase 2: Firm B approaches the same workers.
Design
Field experiment randomizing the wage ($25 vs $65) and the contract: salient noncompete, hidden noncompete, or none (15,000 got no offer). During Firm B's recruiting, Firm A sent a reminder of contractual obligations. Assumption: random assignment. Plausible.
Findings
Salient noncompetes cut opening (−7.9pp) and signing (−2.3pp), equivalent to ≈ $15–44 of wage; hidden had little effect (most spent seconds on that page). Noncompetes cut moving to Firm B only after the reminder. Secret-sharing rare and unaffected.
Model
Explains why noncompetes don't carry a compensating differential: workers often don't notice them. They restrict mobility when salient or enforced ("cage").

(k) Pay transparency and the gender gap (Baker et al. 2023)

Randomized controlled trial

Horton · Cullen et al. (negotiation) · Cowgill et al. · Caldwell & Oehlsen
Idea
Randomly assign treatment, so groups are identical in expectation. ATE ≈ mean(treated) − mean(control).
Key assumption
Randomization worked (check balance) and no spillovers between groups.
Worries
Partial take-up (then it's intention-to-treat); selective reporting of outcomes (Cullen et al. use Lee bounds); spillovers (Horton: treated and control firms share a platform); partial equilibrium and external validity (tech workers, one platform, freelance recruiters).

Instrumental variables

Staiger · Datta · Caldwell & Oehlsen · Townsend & Allan (lottery)
Idea
\(X\) (the wage) and \(Y\) (employment) are confounded. Find \(Z\) that moves \(X\) and affects \(Y\) only through \(X\). \(\beta=\delta/\pi\) = reduced form ÷ first stage.
Assumptions
(1) Relevance: \(Z\) moves \(X\) (check the first-stage F; Datta's are 34–112). (2) As good as random. (3) Exclusion: \(Z\) affects \(Y\) only via \(X\). (4) Monotonicity. Result is a LATE for compliers.
In this unit
To estimate a supply elasticity, the instrument must shift the firm's wage without being a supply shock: a demand shock or an institutional wage change.
  • Staiger: Nurse Pay Act gap. Exclusion fails: rivals' wages rose too.
  • Datta: living-wage accreditation and the HR top-up quirk.
  • Caldwell & Oehlsen: randomized pay offers instrument realized earnings.
  • Townsend & Allan: lottery win instruments residence (first stage ≈ 0.7).

Difference-in-differences

Prager & Schmitt · Cengiz et al. · Johnson et al. · Baker et al. · Cullen & P-H · Townsend & Allan (list)
Idea
\[(Y_{T,after}-Y_{T,before})-(Y_{C,after}-Y_{C,before})\]. Two-way fixed effects: \(Y_{it}=\alpha_i+\delta_t+\beta T_{it}+u_{it}\).
Key assumption
Parallel trends: without treatment, treated and control would have moved together. Check pre-period event-study coefficients (normalize the year before to 0).

Q1 · Wage-setting in a monopsonistic market

One firm, workers \(i\in[0,N]\), \(y=AL\) with \(A=p=1\). Utility \(C-ev_i\); consumption = wage if employed, 0 if not. \(F(x)=P[v_i<x]=x^\alpha\) on \([0,1]\).

Q1aWhat is the efficient employment rate: for what share of workers is MRPL greater than their distaste for work?
Solution

MRPL \(=pA=1\). Every \(v_i\in[0,1]\) is below 1, so working creates surplus for all workers: efficient employment rate = 100% (\(P[v_i<1]=1^\alpha=1\)).

Q1bDerive the elasticity of labor supply and show it doesn't depend on employment.
Solution

Work iff \(w-v_i\ge0\iff v_i\le w\). So \(L^s(w)=N\,P[v_i\le w]=Nw^\alpha\). Logs: \(\log L^s=\log N+\alpha\log w\), so \(\eta=\frac{\partial\log L^s}{\partial\log w}=\alpha\). A constant: it doesn't depend on \(w\) or \(L\).

Q1cUnder wage-posting, what wage does the firm pick?
Solution

Markdown formula: \[w=\text{MRPL}\cdot\frac{1}{1+1/\eta}=1\cdot\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}=\boxed{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\].

Check by direct maximization: \(\pi(w)=(1-w)Nw^\alpha\). FOC: \[-Nw^\alpha+(1-w)N\alpha w^{\alpha-1}=0\Rightarrow w=\alpha(1-w)\Rightarrow w=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\]. ✓

Q1dEmployment under posting as a function of α. Compute it for α = 0.5, 1, 2. Surprising?
Solution

\(L^{post}=N\big(\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\big)^\alpha\).

αPosted wageEmployment / N
0.51/3 ≈ 0.333\((1/3)^{0.5}\approx\) 0.577
11/2\((1/2)^1=\) 0.500
22/3 ≈ 0.667\((2/3)^2\approx\) 0.444

Surprising: a higher \(\alpha\) means more elastic supply and a smaller markdown, yet employment falls, the opposite of the usual intuition that more elastic supply means less monopsony distortion.

What's going on: \(\alpha\) does two things at once. It is the elasticity, and it also shifts the whole distribution of distaste: \(P[v<x]=x^\alpha\) puts more workers near \(v=1\) as \(\alpha\) rises. So at any wage below 1, fewer workers are willing. The wage rises toward 1, but \(w^\alpha\) still falls. (As \(\alpha\to\infty\), employment → \(N/e\approx0.37N\); as \(\alpha\to0\), → \(N\).) Since efficient employment is always 100%, the employment loss from posting grows with \(\alpha\) here. Lesson: you can't change η in this model without changing who the workers are.

Q1eNow the firm bargains and each employed worker gets their reservation wage. Find \(w^r(v_i)\), the average wage \(\int_0^1w^r(v)f(v)\,dv\), and compare it to the posted wage.
Solution

Reservation wage: \(w^r(v_i)=v_i\). All workers have \(v_i\le1=\) MRPL, so all are hired (efficient, 100%).

Density \(f(v)=\alpha v^{\alpha-1}\). Average wage:

\[ \int_0^1 v\cdot\alpha v^{\alpha-1}\,dv=\alpha\int_0^1v^\alpha\,dv=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}. \]

Exactly the posted wage. Same average pay, but bargaining employs everyone (vs \(N(\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha})^\alpha\)) and pays them different amounts. So bargaining is efficient and more unequal; posting is equal but loses jobs. That's "EIS" in one problem. (With Nash weight \(\beta>0\), as in L8, the bargaining average rises to \(\beta+(1-\beta)\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\).)

Q2 · Gender and markdowns

A CRS firm with MRPL = 10 employs men and women; \(\eta_{men}=4\), \(\eta_{women}=2\).

Q2aUsing the three reasons firms face low elasticities, give one reason women might be less elastic than men.
Solution

Any one of "Picky, Blind, Few":

  • Picky (heterogeneous preferences): if women place more weight on a short commute or flexibility (e.g. childcare), fewer jobs suit them, so they're less likely to leave over pay. Mas & Pallais find women value work-from-home more.
  • Few (concentration): commuting constraints shrink the set of feasible employers.
  • Blind (search frictions): narrower job-search networks mean fewer known alternatives.

Note: Caldwell & Oehlsen find that among Uber drivers women are not less elastic to their employer.

Q2bIf the firm posts separate wages for men and women, what are they?
Solution

Men: \(10\cdot\frac{4}{5}=\boxed{8}\). Women: \(10\cdot\frac{2}{3}\approx\boxed{6.67}\). The less elastic group gets the bigger markdown.

Q2cWith one wage for both, show \(\eta=s_{men}\eta_{men}+s_{women}\eta_{women}\). Which wage if the shares are 0.5/0.5? If \(s_{men}=0.1\)?
Solution

\(L(w)=L_m(w)+L_w(w)\), so

\[ \eta=\frac{w}{L}\big(L_m'+L_w'\big)=\frac{L_m}{L}\cdot\frac{wL_m'}{L_m}+\frac{L_w}{L}\cdot\frac{wL_w'}{L_w}=s_m\eta_m+s_w\eta_w. \]

Shares 0.5/0.5: \(\eta=0.5(4)+0.5(2)=3\), so \(w=10\cdot\frac34=\boxed{7.5}\).

\(s_{men}=0.1\): \(\eta=0.1(4)+0.9(2)=2.2\), so \(w=10\cdot\frac{2.2}{3.2}=\boxed{6.875}\). A mostly-female workforce means a lower common wage.

Q3 · Caldwell & Oehlsen

Q3aWhy do the authors use IV? What assumptions does their instrument need, and are they satisfied?
Solution

They want the elasticity of drivers' hours with respect to their pay. Realized hourly earnings aren't random: they depend on when and where drivers choose to work and on demand shocks that also affect hours, so OLS is biased. Uber's randomized pay offers shift earnings exogenously, so they instrument realized pay.

  • Relevance: the offer raises earnings. Testable; yes.
  • As good as random: offers were randomized. Yes.
  • Exclusion: the offer affects hours only through pay, not e.g. by reminding drivers about the app or signaling something about future pay. Mostly plausible.
  • Monotonicity: a higher offer never reduces anyone's pay. Plausible.
Q3bHow does competition from Lyft affect the labor supply elasticity facing Uber? Do we need extra assumptions?
Solution

Access to Lyft roughly doubles the elasticity of supply to Uber: with an alternative app, a pay cut sends drivers to Lyft, not just home. The implied markdown falls from ≈68% to ≈50%.

Extra assumption: whether a driver can use Lyft is not randomized, so we need Lyft access to be unrelated to other driver traits that affect their elasticity (as good as random). Drivers who can drive for Lyft might differ in car type, location or commitment to driving.

Q3cRelate the gender-specific estimates to Q2. Do they suggest the gender wage gap comes from different markdowns?
Solution

Q2 shows a gap arises if women are less elastic to the firm. Caldwell & Oehlsen find women are about twice as elastic as men to the market (in and out of work), but no less elastic to their employer. The markdown depends on the firm-specific elasticity, so they give no reason for Uber to mark women down more. Different markdowns don't explain the gender gap here. (Uber's pay is also set by a gender-blind algorithm.)

Part A · The official practice question (≈ 35 min)

Setup

Workers \(i\in[0,N]\) choose to work at firm \(F\) or be unemployed. Utility \(u_i(C,e)=C-a_ie\); no income if not employed, so \(C_i\le we_i\). Disutility \(F(x)=P[a_i\le x]=x^\eta\) for \(0\le x\le1\) (assume less than full employment). The firm has \(y_F(L)=AL^\rho\), \(A>0\), \(0<\rho<1\), output price \(p=1\).

a)Derive a worker's reservation wage and the labor supply schedule (for a wage between 0 and 1).
Solution

Work iff \(w-a_i\ge0\), so \(w^r_i=a_i\). \(L^s(w)=N\,P[a_i\le w]=Nw^\eta\).

b)Find the firm's marginal revenue product of labor.
Solution

\(\text{MRPL}=\rho AL^{\rho-1}\).

c)Competitive equilibrium (firm takes wages as given): find employment and the wage.
Solution

\[w=\rho A(Nw^\eta)^{\rho-1}\Rightarrow w^{1-\eta(\rho-1)}=\rho AN^{\rho-1}\Rightarrow w=\big(\rho AN^{\rho-1}\big)^{\frac{1}{1-\eta(\rho-1)}}\]. Employment \[L=Nw^\eta=N\big(\rho AN^{\rho-1}\big)^{\frac{\eta}{1-\eta(\rho-1)}}\].

d)A minimum wage \(\underline w\) above the competitive wage is imposed; the firm still takes the wage as given. Find employment.
Solution

Above the competitive wage there's excess supply, so employment is on labor demand: \[\underline w=\rho AL^{\rho-1}\Rightarrow L=\big(\frac{\underline w}{\rho A}\big)^{\frac{1}{\rho-1}}\].

e)Wage-posting equilibrium without a minimum wage: solve for the profit-maximizing wage, \(\max_w\{p\,y_F(L^s(w))-wL^s(w)\}\).
Solution

Objective: \(A(Nw^\eta)^\rho-wNw^\eta=AN^\rho w^{\rho\eta}-Nw^{\eta+1}\). FOC: \[\rho\eta AN^\rho w^{\rho\eta-1}-(\eta+1)Nw^\eta=0\Rightarrow w=\Big(\frac{(\eta+1)N^{1-\rho}}{\rho\eta A}\Big)^{\frac{1}{\rho\eta-1-\eta}}\].

Shortcut check ("competition with a shrunk A"): \(w^{1+\eta-\eta\rho}=\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}\rho AN^{\rho-1}\). Flip the base and the exponent's sign and it's the same expression.

f)Wage-posting with the same minimum wage as in (d): what is employment?
Solution

The minimum is above the competitive wage, so there's excess supply; the firm hires until MRPL = \(\underline w\): the same \(L=\big(\frac{\underline w}{\rho A}\big)^{\frac{1}{\rho-1}}\) as in (d). Above the competitive wage, posting and competition give identical answers.

g)Under wage-posting, what minimum wage maximizes employment?
Solution

The competitive wage from (c). Below it, raising the minimum moves employment up the supply curve; above it, down the demand curve. "Between the wages, jobs go up," peaking at \(w_{comp}\).

Question 1 · Minimum wages under wage posting

Setup

Workers \(i\in[0,N]\), utility \(C-a_ie\), no income if not working, \(a_i\sim U[0,1]\). One firm with \(y=AL\), \(A=0.8\), price 1.

  1. Derive labor supply and its elasticity.
  2. Find the competitive wage and employment.
  3. Find the posted wage and employment.
  4. A minimum wage of 0.6 is imposed. Employment under posting? Under the competitive model?
  5. The minimum is raised to 0.9. Employment now?
  6. Which minimum wage maximizes employment under posting?
  7. Cengiz et al. find state minimum wages don't reduce employment. Which model does that support? Name one piece of evidence that complicates your answer.
Solution
  1. Work iff \(w\ge a_i\); \(L^s=N\,P[a_i\le w]=Nw\). \(\log L=\log N+\log w\): \(\eta=1\).
  2. CRS: MRPL \(=A=0.8\) at any \(L\). Competitive: \(w=0.8\), \(L=0.8N\).
  3. Markdown with \(\eta=1\): \(w=0.8\cdot\frac12=0.4\), \(L=0.4N\). (Direct: \(\max_w(0.8-w)Nw\Rightarrow w=0.4\).)
  4. Posting: 0.6 is between 0.4 and 0.8, so the firm pays 0.6 and hires everyone willing: \(L=0.6N\), up from 0.4N. Competitive: 0.6 < 0.8 doesn't bind, so still 0.8N.
  5. 0.9 > MRPL = 0.8: every worker loses the firm money, so with CRS it hires no one (L = 0) in both models. With CRS, demand is flat at A: any minimum above A shuts the firm down.
  6. 0.8, the competitive wage, giving \(L=0.8N\).
  7. Posting: in the competitive model a binding minimum must reduce employment; under posting it can leave it unchanged or raise it. Complication: posting implies output rises and prices fall, but Harasztosi & Lindner find firms pass minimum-wage costs on as higher prices, which fits a competitive model with inelastic demand. (Or: Horton found hiring fell on an online platform.)

Question 2 · Bargaining, posting and pay transparency

Setup

One firm with \(y=L\) (price 1). Workers \(i\in[0,N]\) with utility \(C-v_i\) if employed, \(v_i\sim U[0,1]\), no other income. Before transparency, wages are Nash-bargained with worker strength \(\beta=0.4\): \(w_i=\beta\cdot\text{MRPL}+(1-\beta)v_i\).

  1. What wage does a worker with \(v_i\) receive? Who is employed?
  2. What are the lowest and highest wages paid? The average?
  3. A pay transparency law forces a single posted wage. What wage, and what employment?
  4. Summarize the effects of the law on employment, average wages and inequality.
  5. Cullen & Pakzad-Hurson find right-to-talk laws lowered wages about 2% and didn't change employment. Which predictions match? Why might employment not fall?
Solution
  1. \(w_i=0.4+0.6v_i\). This exceeds \(v_i\) for every \(v_i<1\), so everyone is employed (efficient).
  2. Lowest (\(v=0\)): 0.4. Highest (\(v=1\)): 1.0. Average: \(0.4+0.6\cdot E[v]=0.4+0.6(0.5)=0.7\).
  3. \(L^s=Nw\), \(\eta=1\), so \(w=1\cdot\frac12=0.5\) and \(L=0.5N\).
  4. Employment falls (N → 0.5N); average wage falls (0.7 → 0.5; check \(\beta/(1+\alpha)=0.4/2=0.2\)); inequality falls from a 0.4–1.0 range to zero. "Equal, lower, fewer."
  5. Wage fall matches; more inequality compression where unions are weak matches too. Employment doesn't. The model has one firm and an unemployment option. If workers easily switch between firms but rarely in and out of work, transparency lowers wages without changing total employment.

Question 3 · Markdowns, elasticities and concentration

Parts
  1. A firm's recruitment elasticity is 2.5 and its separation elasticity is −1.5. What's the long-run labor supply elasticity? If MRPL is $25/hour, what does a wage-posting firm pay?
  2. In a survey, 40% of workers are likely to quit with no pay cut and 46% with a 2% pay cut. Implied quit elasticity?
  3. A city's hospitals have employment shares 0.4, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1. Compute the HHI and the "equivalent number" of hospitals. Then the two smallest merge: new HHI?
  4. Under wage posting, what does the merger do to wages and employment? Under bargaining? What did Prager & Schmitt find, and which model fits?
Solution
  1. "Hires minus quits": \(\eta=2.5-(-1.5)=4\). Wage \(=25\cdot\frac{4}{5}=\$20\).
  2. \(\frac{0.46-0.40}{0.40}=15\%\) more quits for a −2% wage: elasticity \(=15/(-2)=-7.5\).
  3. \(0.16+0.09+0.04+0.01=0.30\) (3,000); equivalent ≈ \(1/0.30=3.3\) hospitals. After merging 0.2 and 0.1: \(0.16+0.09+0.09=0.34\) (3,400); ΔHHI \(=2(0.2)(0.1)=0.04\).
  4. Posting: fewer alternatives lower η, so a bigger markdown; the firm moves down its supply curve: wages and employment fall. Bargaining: workers' outside options worsen, so wages fall, but everyone with \(w^r<\) MRPL is still hired: employment unchanged. Prager & Schmitt: big mergers cut skilled wages ≈ 4% and nursing/pharmacy ≈ 7%, with no employment change: bargaining.

Question 4 · Empirics

4.1Describe a paper that asks whether firms set wages by posting or bargaining. (a) Setting (b) Key findings (c) Which model do the results support, and why?
Solution

Townsend & Allan. (a) New Zealand migrants on Essential Skills visas tied to one job. (b) Migrants gaining residence (including via random visa lotteries) switch employers more but don't earn more than same-firm coworkers; when an occupation joins the shortage list, freeing all migrants in it, earnings rise ≈ 2.8% for migrants and residents. (c) Posting: under bargaining an individual's better outside option raises their wage; under posting the wage depends on everyone's options via η, so only the market-wide shock matters.

Also acceptable: Cullen et al.'s negotiation RCT (some bargaining, small average effect), or Prager & Schmitt (bargaining).

4.2Describe a paper that used a difference-in-differences design. (a) Setting (b) Findings (c) What does parallel trends require here, and is it plausible?
Solution

Cengiz, Dube, Lindner & Zipperer. (a) 138 state minimum-wage increases, 1979–2016. (b) Jobs below the new minimum fall by 1.8% of employment, jobs just above rise by 2.1%: no net job loss; affected wages +6.8%. (c) Employment in each wage bin in raising states would have evolved like in other states absent the increase. Plausible: event studies show flat trends before increases; a worry is that states raise minimums when their labor markets are strong.

Also good: Prager & Schmitt, Johnson et al., Baker et al., Cullen & Pakzad-Hurson.

Markdown (L6, L7)

\[\max_w\ p\,y(L^s(w))-wL^s(w)\Rightarrow w=\text{MRPL}\cdot\frac{1}{1+1/\eta}=\text{MRPL}\cdot\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}\].

Why: one more worker means a raise for everyone, so marginal cost = \(w(1+1/\eta)\).

η = 0.1 → 9% · 1 → 50% · 4 → 80% · 4.6 → 82% · 9 → 90% · ∞ → 100%.

Reverse: \(s=w/\text{MRPL}\Rightarrow\eta=s/(1-s)\).

Elasticities

\(\eta=\frac{\partial\log L}{\partial\log w}\). \(L=Nw^\alpha\Rightarrow\eta=\alpha\). \(L=N(w-c)\Rightarrow\eta=\frac{w}{w-c}\).

Long run: \(L=R/S\Rightarrow\eta=\varepsilon_R-\varepsilon_S\) ("hires minus quits"). Datta 3 + 1.6 = 4.6.

Regression: \(\varepsilon_S=\text{coef}/\text{mean rate}\) (−0.08/0.05).

Survey: \(\frac{p_1-p_0}{p_0}\div(-\text{cut})\): 47% → 51% at −1% = −8.5.

OLS fails: supply shocks trace demand. Need an instrument: demand shock or institutional wage change.

Sources of monopsony: "Picky, Blind, Few"

Picky: heterogeneous preferences (Mas & Pallais). Blind: search frictions (≈14 applications, ≈2 interviews; networks). Few: concentration, \(\text{HHI}=\sum s^2\), \(1/N\) if equal, merger \(\Delta=2s_as_b\).

\(L^s=Nw^\eta\), \(y=AL^\rho\), MRPL \(=\rho AL^{\rho-1}\).

Competitive: \(w^{1+\eta-\eta\rho}=\rho AN^{\rho-1}\).

Posting: same with \(A\to A\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}\) ("competition with a shrunk A").

CRS (\(y=AL\)): \(w_{comp}=A\), \(w_{post}=A\frac{\eta}{1+\eta}\).

Bargaining · "EIS"

L7: \(w_i=w^r_i\). L8 Nash: \(w_i=\beta\,\text{MRPL}+(1-\beta)w^r_i\). Hired iff \(w^r_i<\) MRPL (efficient).

\(F=x^\alpha\): \(E[v]=\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\) = posted wage (HW3 Q1e).

Employment: posting too low, bargaining efficient. Inequality: posting one wage. Spillovers: posting wage depends on everyone's options.

Minimum wage: "between the wages, jobs go up"

\(\underline w\le w_{post}\): nothing. \(w_{post}<\underline w\le w_{comp}\): posting \(L=L^s(\underline w)\) ↑; competitive unchanged. \(\underline w>w_{comp}\): both \(\text{MRPL}(L)=\underline w\), ↓. Peak at \(w_{comp}\). CRS: any \(\underline w>A\) shuts the firm.

Pay transparency: "equal, lower, fewer"

Before: avg \(\beta+(1-\beta)\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\), all employed. After (posting): \(\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}\), employment \((\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha})^\alpha\). Fall \(=\frac{\beta}{1+\alpha}\).

Groups (HW3 Q2)

Separate: \(w_g=\text{MRPL}\frac{\eta_g}{1+\eta_g}\) (10 → 8 men, 6.67 women). One wage: \(\eta=\sum s_g\eta_g\) (0.5/0.5 → η = 3, w = 7.5; 0.1 men → 2.2, 6.875).

Noncompetes: "cage versus comp"

Cage: fewer outside options, wages ↓. Comp: compensating differential, wages ↑. Data: cage wins (Johnson et al. −11%); workers don't notice hidden clauses (Cowgill et al.).
